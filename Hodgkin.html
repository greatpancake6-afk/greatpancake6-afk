Question 7 — Kaplan–Meier Analysis

What is a Kaplan-Meier graph? A Kaplan-Meier graph estimates how likely patients are to stay event-free (alive, or free of treatment failure) over time. Some patients in a study are not followed all the way to the event — maybe the study ended, or they moved away. We call this censoring. Censored means we know how long a patient was followed, but we did not see the event happen during that time. A Kaplan-Meier graph can still use these patients' information without guessing what happened to them later. On the graph: the x-axis is time since diagnosis, in years. The y-axis is the probability of still being event-free. The line is flat, then drops in a step every time a patient has the event. Small tick marks on the line show patients who were censored.

Overall Survival vs. Freedom From Treatment Failure

These two graphs are answering different questions. Overall survival asks: is the patient still alive? Freedom from treatment failure asks: has the lymphoma come back or stopped responding to treatment? A patient can have a treatment failure (relapse or progression) and still be alive afterward — they might get more treatment. That is why the freedom-from-treatment-failure line drops lower and faster than the overall-survival line here: patients had a treatment failure, while patients died, in this dataset. This dataset has no comparison group, so these graphs describe what happened to this specific group of patients — they do not by themselves prove that any particular treatment caused a particular result.

Limitations: This graph only shows an association within one group of patients — it is not a controlled experiment, so it cannot prove that Deauville score causes treatment failure. Deauville score itself is partly a reflection of how advanced or aggressive the lymphoma already is, so other unmeasured factors could explain some of this pattern. The Deauville 2 and 3 groups are small, so those two estimates are less certain than the others. No statistical significance test was performed on this relationship, so we only describe the pattern we can see, not whether it is statistically significant.

Data Quality

The original dataset contains 616 patients. Not every graph below uses all 616 — each one uses only the patients who have valid data for what that specific graph needs. The table below summarizes this for every analysis in this dashboard.

  • Deauville score was only counted when the dataset had an actual score of 1–5. Entries such as blank cells, "DX", "No PET", "No EOT PET", "Images N/A", and similar text notes were not treated as scores — they were excluded from Deauville-based graphs and counted as missing.
  • PET/Deauville scoring became more common later in the study period, so patients diagnosed earlier are less likely to have a recorded Deauville score. This is a real feature of the patient group over time, not a data-entry mistake — but it does mean the Deauville-based graphs describe a subset of patients that is skewed toward more recently diagnosed cases.
  • One patient had a negative recorded freedom-from-treatment-failure time, which is not possible in real life. This patient was excluded from the freedom-from-treatment-failure graphs (Graph 7 and Graph 8) but is still included in the overall-survival graph and the Question 6 graphs.
  • Disease stage sub-labels (such as A/B, E, or "bulky") were simplified to just the main stage number (I–IV) for Graph 3. Two patients had no stage value that could be read from the source file and were excluded from that graph only.
